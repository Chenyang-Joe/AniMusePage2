Overview

Give AniMuse a static 3D animal — any mesh, any species, straight out of a scan or a generator — and a sentence describing what it should do. It returns an animated mesh sequence.

There is no rigging step. No skeleton is fitted, no joints are named, no artist marks up the model. AniMuse builds its own deformation handles: a set of oriented Semantic Gaussian Bones (SGBs) that it places inside the mesh, and it generates motion by moving those. One model covers every topology, the motion state is a small set of SGBs rather than tens of thousands of vertices, and SGB k lands on the same body part for a fox as for an elephant — which is the semantic in the name, and what turns an index into something you can grab.

Drag the divider: SGBs on the left, the surface they drive on the right. Drag anywhere else to orbit — both halves share one camera, so they can never fall out of step.

It runs in two stages. Stage 1 — Rigging reads the static mesh and predicts its SGBs: soft, oriented ellipsoids that sit inside the body, together with how strongly each one pulls on each vertex. They are learned end-to-end from mesh sequences alone — no rigged ground truth is ever used, because the training signal is simply whether deforming one frame with them reproduces another.

Stage 2 — Generation generates the trajectory of those SGBs over time with a diffusion model, conditioned on the text prompt and on what Stage 1 learned about each SGB's local geometry. Linear blend skinning turns the SGB trajectory back into an animated mesh. Because the motion state never grows with mesh resolution, long sequences on dense meshes stay tractable — and the handles stay small enough in number to be a usable control surface.

Stage 1The rig

Stage 1 is what removes the artist from the loop. Given a raw mesh it produces a complete deformation setup in a single forward pass: where the handles go, how big and how oriented each one is, and which parts of the surface each one moves.

How good is the rig?

We measure it the way you would judge a rigger: extract the rig from a rest-pose mesh, use it to deform that mesh onto a posed target, and see how far off you are. Stage 2Text to motion

Stage 2 generates the SGBs' trajectory from a sentence. It is trained on roughly 75,000 animal motion clips and evaluated on a benchmark it has never seen — different meshes, a different species distribution, a different capture pipeline.

Eight species, one divider. Every animal here is driven by the same SGB slots — drag the cut to pull the whole wall from the SGBs to the surface they deform.

How good is it?

We ran a 50-participant user study on the out-of-domain benchmark — 30 samples, two-alternative forced choice, following the protocol used in the text-to-motion literature. Against the strongest published skeleton-free baseline, participants preferred AniMuse's result 74.6% of the time overall and 84.0% on whether the motion matched the prompt. Against a skeleton-based baseline the margins are wider still. Against ground-truth capture we are, fairly, still behind — which is the honest state of the problem.

ControlBeyond prompting

A generator you can only prompt is a slot machine. Because AniMuse's SGBs carry a stable meaning, they double as a control surface — and because that meaning is shared across species, a control authored once applies to every animal.

Motion inpainting

Pin some SGBs to a trajectory you want, and the model generates a coherent whole-body motion around them. Below, the pink SGBs are the pinned ones — the same four indices on every species, driven by one shared cadence. Start with Constraint only to see what the user actually supplies, then bring in the SGBs the model wrote around it, then the surface those SGBs drive.

Part-level editing

The same index space gives direct manual control. Below, one slider drags the twelve SGBs that make up the left front leg — and because those twelve mean the same leg on every animal, one slider drives all three species at once, with no per-animal setup.

Top row: the surface. Bottom row: the SGBs driving it. The highlighted slots are the leg group — one slider, three species, no per-animal setup.

The same property makes motion transfer fall out for free: a trajectory authored on one animal is, by construction, already addressed to the right body parts on any other. We show pinning and dragging here; transfer is the same mechanism with the whole trajectory supplied instead of a few slots.

WebAnimal3D

AniMuse trains on raw mesh sequences — no template, no skeleton, no rigging annotation — so the training corpus can come from anywhere. We used that to build WebAnimal3D, an annotation-free animal motion corpus reconstructed from public web video, with an LLM-written caption per clip.

At a glance

Inputone static mesh (any topology, any species) and one sentence
Outputan animated mesh sequence, up to 300 frames per pass
Setup requirednone — no skeleton, no joint names, no skinning weights, no artist pass
Controlpin any subset of the SGBs, or drag a named part; both transfer across species unchanged
Trained on~75k animal motion clips, plus our web-video corpus
Evaluated ona 25-species rigging benchmark, and an out-of-domain text-to-motion benchmark
Known limits300 frames per pass; the topology gate can still bridge two surfaces that touch but are not connected
